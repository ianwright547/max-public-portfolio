# Local competitor analysis SOP

## Purpose

This SOP explains how Max should identify true local search competitors, collect comparable evidence, find policy-safe opportunities, and avoid false causal claims.

## Core principles

- A real-world competitor may not be a search competitor.
- A map competitor may not be an organic competitor.
- One search from one device is not the whole market.
- Competitor activity is evidence of what exists, not proof of why it ranks.
- Copying a competitor is not a strategy.
- Policy violations must not become recommendations.
- Estimates must stay labeled as estimates.

## Required inputs

- client business and location
- approved services
- approved service areas
- target query clusters
- current date
- business priorities
- known competitors
- live result source or approved export
- geogrid data when available
- client profile
- client website
- competitor profiles and websites
- approved tool access

## Step 1: Define the market

Record:

- country
- city or service area
- search radius or grid
- language
- business type
- customer type
- priority services
- query set
- search date
- device or result source
- location used for the search

Do not combine several markets without showing each market separately.

## Step 2: Build the competitor set

Identify candidates from:

- map results
- organic results
- paid results
- local marketplaces
- industry directories
- geogrid scans
- Search Console result research
- client knowledge
- customer mentions

Classify each candidate:

- direct map competitor
- direct organic competitor
- paid competitor
- directory
- marketplace
- informational publisher
- franchise or multi-location brand
- unrelated
- unknown

Select a primary set that appears repeatedly across important queries or locations.

Record why each competitor qualifies.

## Step 3: Sample more than one query and point

Use:

- primary service query
- secondary service queries
- emergency query when relevant
- explicit city query
- implicit local query
- qualification query when relevant
- several geographic points when map visibility matters

Keep the settings reproducible.

Do not call a business the market leader from one result.

## Step 4: Compare Google Business Profiles

Collect visible evidence:

- profile name
- primary category
- additional categories when available
- address or service-area status
- hours
- website destination
- services
- attributes
- review count
- rating
- recent review dates
- owner response activity
- photos
- posts
- questions
- duplicate or related profiles
- profile status

Rules:

- do not assume all categories are visible
- do not infer private profile settings
- do not copy keyword-stuffed names
- do not recommend false hours
- do not instruct customers to place keywords in reviews
- do not treat more reviews as proof of higher rank

## Step 5: Compare website coverage

For each competitor, record:

- homepage purpose
- category pages
- service pages
- physical location pages
- service-area pages
- service and place pages
- blogs
- FAQs
- project pages
- location proof
- internal-link pattern
- structured data
- indexable page count when measured
- title and H1 patterns
- opening-copy patterns
- conversion paths
- technical barriers

Do not equate a larger website with a better website.

Do not create a page-count target from a competitor count.

## Step 6: Compare search intent coverage

Build a matrix:

| Query cluster | Client asset | Competitor assets | Gap | Recommended action |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Classify gaps:

- no client coverage
- weak client coverage
- duplicate client coverage
- competitor-only service
- service not offered by client
- informational gap
- place gap
- qualification gap
- no action

Do not recommend targeting a service the client does not offer.

## Step 7: Compare reviews

Review:

- total count
- rating
- recent review activity
- response rate
- response quality
- recurring service mentions
- recurring complaint themes
- platform spread

Use public review text only within policy and privacy limits.

Do not:

- claim reviews caused a rank
- identify a review as fake without evidence
- recommend copying phrases into customer requests
- recommend review gating
- recommend incentives that violate rules

## Step 8: Compare citations and local authority

Review:

- core platforms
- industry directories
- local associations
- chambers
- supplier pages
- partner pages
- local media
- sponsorships
- professional organizations
- relevant backlinks

Classify opportunities:

- legitimate and applicable
- legitimate but costly
- relationship required
- client not eligible
- low quality
- spam risk
- unknown

Do not recommend a source only because a competitor has it.

## Step 9: Compare technical and entity clarity

Review:

- crawl access
- status codes
- canonicals
- sitemap
- structured data
- business identity
- location identifiers
- phone and address alignment
- page index status
- raw and rendered content

A competitor's valid markup does not prove that markup caused visibility.

## Step 10: Identify policy violations carefully

Possible observations may include:

- name stuffing
- fake or unclear location
- duplicate profiles
- misleading service claims
- copied pages
- suspicious review patterns
- link spam

Rules:

- save evidence
- use neutral language
- distinguish confirmed policy conflict from suspicion
- do not accuse a person or company of fraud without proof
- do not report competitors automatically
- require approval before any platform report
- do not imitate the violation

## Step 11: Build the opportunity matrix

For each opportunity, record:

- gap
- client eligibility
- evidence
- competitor evidence
- customer value
- policy risk
- effort
- confidence
- recommendation
- approval
- verification
- priority

Opportunity classes:

- correct a client error
- complete an approved profile field
- create or improve a useful page
- strengthen internal links
- build a legitimate local relationship
- improve review operations
- fix a technical problem
- create a useful blog
- improve measurement
- measured test
- no action

## Step 12: Explain rank differences safely

Use language such as:

- "The competitor is closer to several measured search points."
- "The competitor uses a category that more closely matches this query."
- "The competitor has a dedicated page for this intent."
- "The competitor appears on several relevant local sources."
- "These are possible contributing differences, not confirmed causes."

Avoid:

- "They rank because they have more reviews."
- "Their schema put them first."
- "The page count caused the result."
- "Copying their title will move the client into the top three."

## Step 13: Produce the report

Use `templates/local-seo/local-competitor-analysis-report.md`.

Include:

- scope
- competitor selection
- map results
- organic results
- profile comparison
- page comparison
- review comparison
- authority comparison
- technical comparison
- opportunity matrix
- prohibited or rejected tactics
- priorities
- measurement plan
- limitations

Run the Universal Human Writing SOP.

## Completion standard

The analysis is complete when:

- the market and query set are reproducible
- map and organic competitors are separated
- the primary competitor set has a reason
- comparisons use dated evidence
- observations and causes are separated
- client-ineligible services are excluded
- policy violations are not copied
- recommendations have a measurement plan
- the final report passed the Human Writing SOP
